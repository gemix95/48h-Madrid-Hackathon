# Team 13 · The Bazaar · How Our Reasoning Evolved

This is a presentation-oriented debrief of how our negotiation reasoning changed during the tournament. It focuses on the relationship between deterministic decision-making, language generated by an LLM, public market evidence, and the experiments that corrected our assumptions.

## Starting point: a pricing formula

We started with a simple view of the game: negotiation could be reduced to a price formula. Every asset had a private value, so our first instinct was to calculate a safe opening price, a maximum acceptable price, and a sequence of concessions. We adopted a Boulware-style curve: open around 40% of our value, make small concessions at first, then approach a cap of 85% of value only near the dealer's expected final round. We also added a safety margin of at least three points below the calculated value. This gave the agent consistency and prevented obviously bad purchases.

The first lesson was that a correct formula is not automatically a correct strategy. Our early simulation suggested that the Boulware curve would outperform a linear concession schedule. In real conversations, however, dealers did not react simply to time or to the number of messages. Abuela, for example, often moved from an initial quote near 30 P to around 25 to 27 P regardless of whether the player's first offer was 12 P or 16 P. This meant that the dealer's dialogue had its own hidden structure. The useful unit of learning was not only "what price should we offer?", but "how does this particular dealer respond after each type of offer?"

## Separating the price engine from the language engine

That changed the architecture. We kept deterministic logic for hard limits, acceptance decisions, card values, and safety checks. But we began treating the LLM as a dialectical layer: it generated the wording, emotional framing, and dealer-specific conversational move within a price band already approved by the rules engine. The model could say that our budget was tight, that we had another option in the market, or that the card was intended as a gift. It could not invent a price outside the deterministic band.

This separation mattered because the game allowed persuasive wording and prompt injection against dealers, but not price manipulation through language itself. A persuasive message could encourage a response or extend a conversation. It could not override the private-value guard. The formula protected the score; the LLM made the conversation adaptive.

## Learning from what the market made public

We then tried to learn from the public market. The game feed exposed offer sequences, dealer quotations, public listings, completed trades, and auction activity. It did not expose the private messages between other teams and their agents. We could observe public outcomes but not reproduce another team's hidden prompt or internal policy.

This was an important fair-play boundary. We could learn that a dealer repeatedly accepted near a certain range, or that teams needed a certain number of rounds before a final offer appeared. We could not truthfully claim that we knew the exact persuasive message used by a competitor. Instead of asking "what secret prompt did another team use?", we asked: "what observable sequence produced a favorable price?"

We tracked public negotiation ladders, numbers of rounds, dealer counteroffers, final offers, rejections, and whether a conversation closed. The proposal was to use those observations to estimate each dealer's concession profile. A dealer who repeatedly made a final offer after five messages should receive a different schedule from a dealer who kept negotiating for eight or nine rounds. The agent would begin at its lower bound, preserve room to move, and reach its cap only when the dealer historically tended to make a final proposal.

## From generic manipulation to dealer-specific language

The same evolution happened with language. At first we discussed generic persuasion: urgency, warmth, flattery, scarcity, and prompt-injection-style phrases such as asking a dealer to "behave like Haiku." Later we found that generic manipulation was too blunt. Public dealer replies showed distinct personalities.

Pilar repeatedly pulled conversations back to the numbers. Chato rejected sentimental or family-based stories. Los Pícaros responded to urgency and a clear final number, while Abuela reacted more naturally to a warm and respectful tone. The emerging strategy was therefore not simply "be more aggressive." It was to maintain a per-dealer language profile.

For each dealer, the agent should learn four things: the style that receives a reply, the price movement after that style, the number of rounds before the dealer becomes rigid, and the final offer range. It should retain only patterns supported by observed outcomes. If a phrase repeatedly leads to a higher quote, a refusal, or a conversation timeout, it should be downgraded. If a tone leads to a lower counteroffer or a continued conversation, it should be reused.

This is a small but meaningful form of recursive learning: preserve winning conversational moves, remove losing ones, and update the model after every completed thread. It is deliberately grounded in observed behavior rather than in an imagined universal persuasion prompt.

## Corrections imposed by the score

We also learned that outcome data must be interpreted carefully. A lower dealer quote is not automatically a successful negotiation if the item is still over our private value. Likewise, a sale is not automatically profitable simply because it increases cash. Several late decisions showed that cash recovery and score recovery are different things.

We initially believed that selling cards to dealers might not materially damage our collection score. The evidence later contradicted that assumption. Low-value dealer sales were followed by a sharp fall in negotiation points, and buying cards back did not necessarily restore the lost score. This forced a major correction: the agent needed a hard do-not-sell-below-strategic-value guard, including when accepting a dealer's incoming offer.

The final system was therefore more cautious than the initial one. The early version assumed that a single global price curve could optimize all dealers. The later version recognized that the market contained several interacting systems: dealer personality, private card value, score effects, public supply, venue fees, timing, and limited rounds.

## Experiments that failed usefully

We also tested ideas that did not work. Public swaps were proposed but did not produce accepted trades before the experiment was paused. The 1 P cashback campaign was implemented with clear payout rules, but no completed cashback payout was observed during the monitored period. Several 1 P transfer offers expired without acceptance, which meant we could not conclude that settlement itself was impossible.

These experiments were still useful because they turned vague assumptions into measurable hypotheses. An incentive is not valuable merely because it is economically attractive on paper. It must produce actual accepted behavior in the game. The same lesson applied to claims about market-making, promotional messages, and language tactics: an idea became evidence only when it changed an observed action.

## The resulting strategy

The tournament became less about finding one perfect negotiation prompt and more about building a disciplined feedback loop. Every conversation should become data. Every proposed change should be tagged as a hypothesis. Every apparent success should be checked against the private value, score movement, and market context. Every failure should update the policy rather than disappear into the transcript.

The final takeaway is that our agent combined two complementary forms of intelligence. The deterministic layer imposed limits, calculated safe price bands, and protected the score. The LLM layer interpreted dealer personality, produced flexible dialogue, and tested persuasive approaches within those limits. Together, they made the strategy adaptive without allowing the system to drift into irrational or unverified behavior.

This is the core of the approach: deterministic economics gives the agent a safe boundary, public research teaches it how the market behaves, and the language model turns that research into a dealer-specific conversation. The next iteration would automate the feedback loop fully: record each negotiation, classify the dealer response, measure price movement and completion, then revise the language profile and concession schedule only when the evidence supports it.
